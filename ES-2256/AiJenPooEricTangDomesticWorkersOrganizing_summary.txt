### 1. Title and Authors
* **Chapter Title:** "Center Stage: Domestic Workers Organizing in the Global City"
* **Book Title:** *The New Urban Immigrant Workforce: Innovative Models for Labor Organizing* (Eds. Sarumathi Jayaraman & Immanuel Ness, M.E. Sharpe, 2005)
* **Authors:** Ai-jen Poo and Eric Tang

---

### 2. Core Definitions and Conceptualizations of Labor
* **Reproductive and Infrastructural Labor:** Domestic work (housekeeping, childcare, eldercare, cooking, personal assisting) is defined not as peripheral work, but as the foundational "key link" of the global corporate economy. It produces the disposable time and convenience that allows the global city’s elite to generate wealth.
* **The "Domestic Sweatshop":** The home is conceptualized as an invisible, unregulated workplace characterized by extreme isolation, surveillance, sub-minimum wages, long hours, lack of privacy, and susceptibility to verbal, physical, and sexual abuse.
* **Racialized and Gendered Labor:** The authors argue that domestic labor is systematically degraded because it is coded as "women’s work" and performed overwhelmingly by women of color.
* **Transnational Indentured Labor:** Labor is viewed through the lens of global capitalism and state immigration controls. Visa policies (such as B1, A3, and G5 visas) tie workers directly to individual employers, enabling employers to hold passports, dictate movement, and enforce debt bondage, creating forms of modern indentured servitude.

### 3. Historical Arguments
* **Roots in Chattel Slavery:** The authors trace the exploitation, flexibility, and societal devaluation of domestic work directly to the legacy of American chattel slavery and the antebellum period, where domestic labor was institutionalized as forced, unpaid labor by Black women.
* **Racial Division and the "Transition to Whiteness":** At the turn of the 20th century, European immigrant women (notably Irish women) entered domestic servitude, driving out native-born white women. However, because domestic work had become socially defined as "Black women’s work," Irish and other white-working-class women strategically abandoned domestic service for industrial and factory sweatshops to distance themselves from racialized labor and assimilate into American "whiteness."
* **Intentional Legislative Exclusion (The New Deal):** The chapter details how domestic and agricultural workers were deliberately excluded from landmark 20th-century protections—most notably the National Labor Relations Act (NLRA) of 1935 and Title VII anti-discrimination thresholds—specifically because these workforces were dominated by African American, Chicano, Filipino, and Chinese workers.
* **Post-Civil Rights Demographic Shift:** In the 1960s and 1970s, civil rights struggles opened unionized public-sector, social service, and institutional healthcare jobs to African American women. To fill the domestic labor vacuum, global cities drew in immigrant women from the Global South (the Philippines, the West Indies, and Latin America).
* **Imperialism, Free Trade, and Forced Migration:** The authors argue that modern migration into domestic work is not purely a personal choice, but a forced outcome of U.S. militarized imperialism, structural adjustment programs by the World Bank/IMF, and Free Trade Zones (FTZs) in the Global South that displace rural economies and generate mass female labor migration.
