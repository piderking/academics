# Hawaiʻi, Guam, and Puerto Rico: The 1898 Imperial Nexus

## 1. How the U.S. Acquired Hawaiʻi: The Anatomy of an Illegal Overthrow

Unlike territories seized from European empires during international warfare, **Hawaiʻi was an independent, internationally recognized sovereign kingdom** that was overthrown from within through collusion between white American commercial interests and U.S. armed forces.

### Chronology of Dispossession:
1. **Sovereign Constitutional Monarchy**:
   * Prior to 1893, the Hawaiian Kingdom was a sovereign nation with its own constitution, legislature, voting system, and international treaties recognized by global powers (including the United States, Great Britain, and France).
2. **Sugar Planters & The 1893 Armed Coup**:
   * White American and European missionary descendants and sugar barons (the "Committee of Safety," organized by Sanford B. Dole, Lorrin Thurston, and families like Castle & Cooke) sought annexation to eliminate U.S. tariffs on imported sugar.
   * On **January 17, 1893**, with the unauthorized collusion of U.S. Minister John L. Stevens, armed U.S. Marines from the *USS Boston* landed in Honolulu and surrounded ʻIolani Palace.
   * To prevent the slaughter of her people, **Queen Liliʻuokalani yielded her executive authority under formal protest**, appealing to the U.S. President and international law to restore the rightful government.
3. **President Cleveland Calls It "An Act of War"**:
   * President Grover Cleveland dispatched investigator James Blount, whose official inquiry (The Blount Report) concluded the coup was an unlawful conspiracy aided by U.S. military intimidation against the overwhelming will of the Hawaiian population.
   * Cleveland termed the invasion *"an act of war"* and refused to annex Hawaiʻi. The coup leaders defied Cleveland, declared the "Republic of Hawaii," and waited for expansionist President William McKinley.
4. **The 1897 Kūʻē Petitions & The 1898 Annexation Loophole**:
   * In 1897, Native Hawaiians organized the historic **Kūʻē Petitions**, gathering signatures from over **21,000 Native Hawaiians** (representing more than half of the total population and nearly the entire adult native citizenry) opposing annexation. 
   * As a result of these petitions, the formal Treaty of Annexation **failed to reach the required two-thirds majority in the U.S. Senate**.
   * **The Newlands Resolution (1898)**: During the outbreak of the Spanish-American War in 1898, the U.S. military prioritized Pearl Harbor as a strategic coaling station and staging ground for operations in the Philippines. To bypass constitutional treaty requirements, Congress annexed Hawaiʻi through a simple domestic majority vote via the **Newlands Resolution (July 7, 1898)**—an act without international legal precedent for acquiring a foreign sovereign nation.

## 3. Key Ethnic Studies Frameworks

### 1. Imperialism as a Material Process of Capitalism
* As articulated in the Week 4 lecture, imperialism is not an abstract concept or moral stance ("vibes"); it is a **material process driven by capitalist expansion**. 
* The acquisition of Hawaiʻi, Guam, and Puerto Rico in 1898 provided:
  1. Captive agricultural markets (sugar, fruit, tobacco).
  2. Deep-water ports and coaling stations for maritime trade routes to Asia and Latin America.
  3. Sovereign nodes for power projection across the Pacific and Atlantic Oceans.

### 2. Settler Colonialism vs. Classical Colonialism
* **Guam & Puerto Rico**: Functioned under classical colonial administration where outside military governors and federal commissions ruled without large-scale permanent white replacement, later governed under the racist doctrine of the *Insular Cases* ("belonging to, but not a part of" the U.S.).
* **Hawaiʻi**: Functioned under **settler colonialism**, where foreign settlers established permanent dominance, privatized communal land (*The Māhele*), dismantled indigenous governance, imported disenfranchised plantation labor, and eventually incorporated the territory into domestic statehood to permanently subsume Kanaka Maoli sovereignty.

### 3. Sovereignty vs. Civil Rights Representation
* For **immigrant communities within Asian America**, racial justice is often framed around **civil rights, civic belonging, and equality under U.S. law**.
* For **Kanaka Maoli (and Chamorros)**, justice cannot be resolved through civil rights alone because the fundamental grievance is the **theft of land and sovereignty**. Demanding equality within the U.S. system treats an occupied sovereign people as mere domestic minorities rather than an independent nation awaiting decolonization.
