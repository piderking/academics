# Week 5: "Americanization", Labor Struggle, and the Politics of "Yellow" Identity

## 1. Synthesis Overview: The "American" in Asian American

## 2. Source Breakdown & Key Concepts

### A. Week 5 Lecture (Instructor: `jmed`)
* **1790 Naturalization Act**: The inaugural Congress restricted naturalized U.S. citizenship to *"any alien, being a free white person."* This established a racial boundary on citizenship that endured for over 160 years (until the McCarran-Walter Act of 1952).
* **The "Americanization" Industry (1910s–1920s)**:
  * Analyzes historical texts like *A Stake in the Land* (Peter A. Speek, 1921), funded by the Carnegie Corporation.
  * Shows how public schools, rural settlement policies, and **public libraries** were intentionally deployed as ideological instruments to "indoctrinate" immigrants into Anglo-conformity.
  * Meanwhile, Asian and non-white laborers were welcomed for their muscle (clearing swamps, building railroads, harvesting fields) but legally barred from owning land (Alien Land Laws) or gaining citizenship.
* **Carlos Bulosan's Local History**:
  * Bulosan and his brother Lorenzo worked as dishwashers at the historic **Madonna Inn in San Luis Obispo** (just miles from Cal Poly), grounding the transnational struggle of Filipino migrant labor in local California geography.
* **The Instrumentalization of Labor**:
  * Draws on Vijay Prashad’s *The Karma of Brown Folk* to demonstrate how immigrant bodies are valued by capitalism strictly as economic instruments ("means to an end"), yet politically excluded and used as racial wedges against Black Americans.

### B. Primary Source: Carlos Bulosan’s "Be American" (p. 258)
* **The Protagonist (Consorcio)**:
  * Arrives from the Philippines with starry-eyed enthusiasm for the "American Dream."
  * Initially equates "being American" with **consumer habits and superficial aesthetics**: buying an American-tailored suit, wearing expensive shoes, eating apples, drinking coffee, and carrying books.
* **The Colonial Paradox of the Filipino Immigrant**:
  * Under U.S. imperial rule following the 1898 Spanish-American War, Filipinos were legally designated **"U.S. Nationals"**—subject to the authority of the U.S. flag, but denied citizenship rights and barred from naturalization under the 1790 Act.
* **The Radical Shift**:
  * Confronting the brutal reality of low-wage migrant labor in Alaskan salmon canneries, California vineyards, and urban dishwashing jobs—alongside rampant anti-Filipino mob violence and anti-miscegenation laws—Consorcio’s understanding matures.
  * He learns to read and write English, joins the agricultural labor movement, and starts a progressive underground union newspaper.
* **Core Takeaway**:
  * Consorcio concludes that **"to be American" is not about a legal passport granted by an imperial state, nor is it about buying consumer goods.** 
  * It is an **active, collective, ongoing struggle for human dignity, workers' rights, and social justice.**

### C. NPR Code Switch: "If We Called Ourselves Yellow" (Kat Chow)
* **The Scientific Fabrication of "Yellow"**:
  * In 1735, Swedish taxonomist **Carl Linnaeus** divided humanity into four color-coded varieties in *Systema Naturae*.
  * Initially labeled Asians *fuscus* (dark/tawny), but in 1758 revised it to ***luridus***—meaning "pale yellow," "ghastly," or "melancholy." This pseudo-scientific color was explicitly invented to rank Europeans (designated "white, gentle, inventive") above Asians (designated "yellow, melancholy, greedy").
* **The "Yellow Peril" (*Gelbe Gefahr*)**:
  * Popularized in the late 19th century by Germany's Kaiser Wilhelm II and spread through American yellow journalism.
  * Portrayed Asians not as distinct cultures, but as an existential, diseased, and hyper-fertile horde threatening to overwhelm Western Christendom (immortalized in characters like Dr. Fu Manchu and racist advertisements like the "Magic Washer" detergent showing Uncle Sam kicking Chinese laborers out).
* **The 1968–1969 Reclamation: "Yellow Power"**:
  * Directly inspired by the **Black Power Movement** (Black Panther Party, Stokely Carmichael).
  * **Birth of "Asian American"**: In 1968 at UC Berkeley, student activists **Yuji Ichioka** and **Emma Gee** founded the **Asian American Political Alliance (AAPA)**, explicitly rejecting the colonial slur "Oriental" to forge a multi-ethnic political coalition.
  * **Amy Uyematsu's 1969 Manifesto**: *"The Emergence of Yellow Power in America"* (published in *Gidra*):
    * Critiqued the **"Model Minority" myth** as a psychological cage designed to win white approval while silencing Asian American grievances and dividing them from Black and Brown liberation movements.
    * Called for "Yellow Power" as an assertion of racial pride, self-determination, and solidarity with the Third World Liberation Front (TWLF).
* **Why "Yellow" Faded While "Asian American" Stayed**:
  1. **Regional Exclusion**: "Yellow" was historically applied almost exclusively to East Asians (Chinese, Japanese, Korean), alienating South Asians and many Southeast Asians who identify as "Brown."
  2. **Pejorative Legacy**: Yellow carried deep historical associations with cowardice, illness, and slur-based trauma.
  3. **Institutional Adoption**: "Asian American" became the recognized civic and demographic category, though it remains in productive tension with Indigenous and Pacific Islander identities (as explored in Week 4!).

## 3. Comparative Matrix: Assimilation vs. Resistance

| Concept | Assimilationist / Colonial Framework | Ethnic Studies / Liberation Framework |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **"Americanization"** | Top-down conformity (Carnegie model); learning English to be compliant labor; erasing native culture; consumerism. | Bottom-up struggle for civil rights, workplace dignity, and multi-racial solidarity (Consorcio's realization). |
| **Citizenship** | A racialized boundary of state control (1790 Act); an instrument to grant or withhold human rights based on whiteness. | A hollow certificate if detached from economic justice; true belonging is forged through collective organizing. |
| **Racial Labels** | **"Oriental" / "Yellow Peril"**: Western projections of disease, threat, and permanent foreignness. | **"Yellow Power" / "Asian American"**: Reclaimed political identities asserting dignity, self-determination, and cross-racial coalition. |
| **The "Model Minority"** | A tool to demonstrate that non-white groups can "succeed" without challenging white supremacy; a wedge against Black demands. | A psychological trap that demands compliance, silences trauma, and obscures widespread Asian American poverty and exploitation. |
